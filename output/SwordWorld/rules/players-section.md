# Chapter 1: Characters

A character, in the broadest sense, refers to anything that lives (or does not live) in a fictional world. In a narrower sense, it is a human or humanoid race that acts as the player's alter ego (**player character: PC**) and those who interact with PCs (**non-player character: NPC**). In Sword World RPG, player characters — and important NPCs — are specifically referred to as **adventurers**.

## 1.1 Ability Scores

In Sword World RPG, a character's basic characteristics are represented by numbers called **ability scores**. There are six types: dexterity, agility, intelligence, strength, life force, and mental power. The higher the number, the better the ability. Ability scores apply to humans and to non-human adventurer races. Human ability scores range from **4–24** (average 14) for each.

### Dexterity
Dexterity of the adventurer's hands. Relates to tasks that involve the use of one's hands — especially important when wielding weapons in combat.

### Agility
Physical balance and agility. Related to working with the whole body. In combat, important when evading enemy attacks.

### Intelligence
Speed of mind and amount of knowledge. Also covers perception — finding hidden objects, sensing abnormalities in the moment. Important when using magic.

### Strength
Muscular strength. Large weapons and heavy armor require high strength.

### Life Force
How well an adventurer can withstand physical injury. The higher the number, the more robust.

### Mental Power
Willpower and perseverance. Using magic is hard work; one cannot cast much magic without high mental capacity. Also related to magic resistance.

### Loss of Ability Scores and Its Impact
Adventurers often suffer physical and mental injuries and fatigue. This is represented by a loss of **life force** and **mental power** (point reduction). If either falls to 0 or less, the adventurer falls unconscious and cannot act. The other four scores (dexterity, agility, intelligence, strength) are rarely reduced, but if they fall to 0 or less, the adventurer becomes disabled (conscious or not, depending on the situation).

Reductions from wounds or exhaustion recover once the wounds are treated or exhaustion is wiped away.

### Ability Bonuses
Success or failure is determined by a **bonus** derived from the ability score, rather than the score itself. Bonus = ability score ÷ 6 (rounded down). Each is referred to as dexterity bonus, intelligence bonus, etc.

When a score changes — even temporarily — the bonus changes accordingly. **Exception**: temporary reductions do **not** affect bonuses for life force or mental power.

**Table 1-1: Ability Bonuses**

| Ability Score | Bonus |
|---|--:|
| 2–5   | ±0 |
| 6–11  | +1 |
| 12–17 | +2 |
| 18–23 | +3 |
| 24–29 | +4 |

Add +1 for every +6 in ability scores thereafter.

## 1.2 Races

Humans are not the only adventurers in Forcelia. Players may choose human or any of the races below.

### Dwarf
Once members of the fae tribe who lived in the fae world, now fully immersed in the material world; they no longer have the ability to travel between worlds.

About 120 cm tall, round and plump, with bulbous noses. Men often grow long beards; women do not. Hair brown to dark brown; eyes similar. Skin dark, near brown.

They can see in the dark as well as in daylight. Very dexterous, excelling at craftsmanship and blacksmithing. Strong sense of tribalism; worship gods of the earth, chiefly **Braki**, god of smithing. Life span ~200 years.

**Rules:**
1. Can see in the dark.
2. Has any one of the following craftsman general skill at level 5: carpentry, smithing, woodworking, stonemasonry, gem crafting (see 7.3).
3. Unable to learn ancient magic (sorcerer) or spirit magic (shaman).

### Grassrunner
Fae of the meadows. Very curious; many abandoned the fae world to become residents of human society. Have lost the ability to return.

About 1 m tall, appearance of a human child. Slightly pointed ears; otherwise not much different from humans. Hair golden to tan, eyes similar, skin predominantly black or reddish.

Nomadic; stay in a place only to raise a few children, then travel again. No tribes, no cities, cheerful and simple, no gods. Life span ~200 years.

**Rules:**
1. Has 1 level in the thief skill.
2. Has 1 level in the ranger skill.
3. Can communicate with plants and insects.
4. Cannot learn any runes.

### Elf
Fae who live in the forests. Long, slender, pointy ears. About 160 cm tall, slim. Thin body hair (no beards). Hair golden to chestnut, sometimes silver. Skin near white, eyes pale blue or green.

Close to humans as a species — can produce **half-elf** offspring. Closed-off life in the forest; dislike outsiders. Do not believe in any gods.

**High elves** are an elite (ancient) subspecies with true immortality; they avoid contact with humans even more than common elves. Most elves seen in human cities are ordinary (newer species) elves with a ~1000-year life span.

**Rules:**
1. Has 1 level in the shaman skill.
2. Unable to learn holy magic (priest).

### Half-Elf
Born of a human and an elf. Slightly pointed ears but can grow a beard; about human height, more delicate. Life span ~200 years.

Traits differ by upbringing: raised-by-elves → elf-like; raised-by-humans → human-like.

### Changeling
Half-elves are not a single generation of mixed blood — they can have their own offspring, and any child might be a full elf, a half-elf, or a human. Humans with distant elven ancestry sometimes produce elves or half-elves through intermittent inheritance. Such children are called **changelings** and are looked down upon in human society.

## 1.3 Skills

Beyond ability scores, Sword World RPG uses **skills** to describe characters. Skills have a greater impact on actions than ability scores.

### 1.3.1 Skill Basics
Skills are divided into several groups. Each skill has a level from **0–10**. 0 = completely unrelated; 10 = highest proficiency attainable by a normal human.

Skills are named by profession (fighter, thief, merchant, etc.). A character can hold levels in more than one skill. Those with a skill are referred to by its name — "a fighter" rather than "someone with the fighter skill".

### 1.3.2 Skill Types
Two top-level categories: **Adventurer skills** (fighter, thief, priest, sorcerer, shaman, sage, ranger, bard — plus NPC-only dark priest and dragon priest) and **General skills** (merchant, farmer, fisher, sailor, craftsman, king, princess, etc.).

**Table 1-2: Skill Types**

- **Adventurer Skills**
  - **Rune Master skills**: Sorcerer, Shaman, Priest, Dark Priest, Dragon Priest
  - Fighter
  - Thief
  - Ranger
  - Sage
  - Bard
- **General Skills**: Merchant, Farmer, Fisher, Sailor, Craftsman, King, Princess, etc.

### 1.3.3 Adventurer Level
A character's capability as an adventurer is determined by their **adventurer level** — the highest single level among their adventurer skills. Example: priest 1 / fighter 4 / sailor 5 / fisher 2 → adventurer level 4 (sailor and fisher are general skills, ignored).

### 1.3.4 Explanation of Adventurer Skills

#### Fighter
Adept warriors who fight with weapons. Experts in combat, able to use all kinds of weapons and armor.

#### Thief
Useful for theft — pickpocketing, lock-picking — but also for adventure: detecting and disabling traps, listening, observing. Thieves also fight with light weapons and light armor that doesn't impede their movement, with ability comparable to fighters.

#### Sage
Knowledgeable and well-versed in all fields of study (Forcelia's academics are not specialized). Often adventure firsthand rather than stay in their study rooms.

#### Ranger
Expert in outdoor activities and natural hazards. Excellent with projectile weapons; combat ability equal to fighters, especially with projectiles.

#### Bard
Play instruments and sing for a living. Familiar with legends and folklore (often encoded in songs). Have the ability to sing **spellsongs** — magical songs that affect all who hear them.

#### Rune Master
Collective term for sorcerer, shaman, priest, dark priest, and dragon priest — the five skills that use runes and handle magic. Feared by the general public for their magical abilities. (Bards also have magical spellsongs but are not counted as rune masters.)

#### Sorcerer
Wield **high ancient** magic — the magical power of the fallen ancient kingdom of **Kastuul** (which fell over 500 years ago). Much was lost with Kastuul's fall, but some survived. Many sorcerers are also sages; not required, but highly advantageous.

#### Shaman
The world is divided into three: the spirit world, the fae world, and the material world. **Silent spirit** are runes used to communicate with spirits and draw out their powers. Shamans wield diverse spirit magic, from the four major powers (earth, water, fire, wind) to the spirit of humans.

#### Priest
Agents of the gods. In the ancient days, gods divided into light and darkness and waged a great war that cost all gods their physical bodies. Gods now interact with the material world through priests. Priests use **holy prayer** to cast **holy magic**.

#### Dark Priest
Agents of the **wicked gods** (the gods of darkness). Use **demon scream** to cast **dark magic** (essentially equivalent to divine magic). **Players may not choose this skill.**

#### Dragon Priest
Use **dragon roar** — magic that imbues the body with dragon characteristics, with the ultimate goal of transforming into a dragon. Worship dragons and try to live like them. **Players may not choose this skill.**

## 1.4 Character Creation

Prepare a Character Sheet (one is at the end of the rulebook, photocopying permitted for personal use).

### 1.4.1 Choose a Race

Five options: human, dwarf, grassrunner, elf, half-elf.

| Race | Ability strengths | Weaknesses | Skill restrictions |
|------|-------------------|------------|--------------------|
| Human | Most average, no restrictions | — | None — good for beginners |
| Dwarf | Strength, Life Force, Mental Power | Agility, Intelligence | Cannot take sorcerer or shaman |
| Grassrunner | Dexterity, Agility, Mental Power | Strength (very low) | Cannot take any rune master skills; low STR → fighter is weak |
| Elf | Dexterity, Agility, Intelligence, Mental Power | Strength, Life Force | Cannot take priest |
| Half-Elf | Slightly higher Dex/Agi/Int/Mnt; slightly lower Str/Life | Not far from average | Raised by elves → cannot take priest. Raised by humans → no restriction |

### 1.4.2 Determine Ability Scores

Roll dice to determine sub-ability scores A through H, per race.

**Table 1-3: Determining Sub-Ability Scores**

| Sub | Human | Dwarf | Grassrunner | Elf | Half-Elf |
|-----|------|-------|-------------|-----|----------|
| A | 2D | 2D+6 | 1D+6 | 1D+6 | 1D+4 |
| B | 2D | 1/2D | 2D+4 | 1D+6 | 1D+6 |
| C | 2D | 1D+4 | 1D+6 | 1D+6 | 1D+4 |
| D | 2D | 1D   | 1D   | 1D+6 | 1D+6 |
| E | 2D | 1D+4 | 1/2D | 1D   | 1D+2 |
| F | 2D | 1D+6 | 1/2D | 1/2D | 1D+2 |
| G | 2D | 2D+4 | 2D+6 | 1D+4 | 1D+4 |
| H | 2D | 2D+4 | 2D+4 | 1D+6 | 2D   |

Notation: "D" = die. 1D = one die; 2D = two dice; 1/2D = half of one die, **rounded up**. "1D+6" = roll 1D and add 6.

The six ability scores are sums of sub-ability pairs:

| Ability | Formula |
|---------|---------|
| Dexterity    | A + B |
| Agility      | B + C |
| Intelligence | C + D |
| Strength     | E + F |
| Life Force   | F + G |
| Mental Power | G + H |

Once computed, sub-ability scores are never used again in play.

### 1.4.4 Age, Gender, Name

No gender/age-based ability differences exist in Sword World RPG. All characters of all genders and any permitted age have equivalent stats.

#### Age
New adventurers should be "green youths". Suggested age ranges:

| Race | Adventure-start age |
|------|---------------------|
| Human | 15 – ~30 (life span ~100; Alecrast average ~50 due to underdeveloped medicine) |
| Dwarf | ~30+ (considered adult later due to long life) |
| Grassrunner | ~40+ |
| Elf | ~100+ (physical maturity at ~20 but elf society waits) |
| Half-Elf (raised by humans) | 15+ |
| Half-Elf (raised by elves) | ~30+ (forced out of elf society before 100 due to shorter life span) |

#### Gender
Both male and female for all races; no mechanical difference. Role and appearance differences equivalent to humans.

#### Name
Use western-style names (first, then last; optional middle). Japanese/Chinese-style names don't suit the Alecrast setting. Nicknames or aliases are common — especially for thieves.

### 1.4.5 Character Growth via Starting Experience Points

Starting experience points may be spent to acquire or raise **adventurer skills only** (general skills cannot be acquired or raised with XP).

**Table 1-7: Experience Points by Skill (at Character Creation)**

| Target Level | Sorcerer & Sage (both) | Sorcerer (alone) | Sorcerer (with Sage) | Shaman | Fighter / Thief / Priest | Ranger / Sage / Bard |
|-----:|-----:|-----:|-----:|-----:|-----:|-----:|
| 1 | 2000 | 2000 | 1500 | 1500 | 1000 | 500 |
| 2 | — | — | — | 2000 | 1500 | 1000 |
| 3 | — | — | — | — | — | 1500 |

- XP is spent **at each level attained** (cumulatively). Example: acquiring ranger 2 from scratch costs 500 + 1000 = 1500 XP.
- Multiple skills can be acquired if there is enough XP. Unused XP is saved for post-game growth.
- **Sorcerer pairing rule**: a character with neither sorcerer nor sage may spend 2000 XP to gain **both** at level 1. Acquiring sorcerer alone for 1500 XP is **illegal** unless the character already has sage at level 1 (which makes the 1500-XP path legal).
- This table is a prefix of Table 8-1 (full character-growth XP costs, CH8).

Same-shape weapons/armor cost different prices based on required strength. Expressions like `×20+40` use the required strength as the multiplier. Example: a broadsword with required strength 14 costs 14 × 20 + 40 = **320 gamels (G)**.

## 1.4.7 Restrictions on Weapons and Armor Based on Skill

Skills constrain equipment choices. For multi-skill characters, consult each skill — if restricted and unrestricted skills overlap, equipment must conform to the restricted skill to benefit both.

### [ Fighter ]

No restrictions. Match required strength to character strength. Equipment with lower required strength reduces effectiveness; higher cannot be used.

### [ Thief ]

To benefit from thief skill, weapons and armor must be light — **required strength up to half the character's strength (rounded up)**. **Metal armor is forbidden except ring mail**, regardless of strength.

### [ Ranger ]

Armor must be light: **required strength up to half the character's strength (rounded up)**. **Metal armor is forbidden except ring mail**.

No weapon restrictions — match required strength to strength. Ranger grants projectile proficiency but no melee weapon proficiency, so projectiles (throwable, made for throwing, shooting) are preferred.

### [ Sorcerer ]

Armor limited to **cloth or soft leather** — take the highest required strength within the character's strength range. **No shield.**

Must purchase a **mage's staff** (required for ancient magic). If strength ≤ 10, match the staff's required strength to the character's strength; if strength ≥ 10, use a staff with required strength 10.

### [ Shaman ]

Armor limited to **cloth, soft leather, or hard leather** — take the highest required strength within the character's strength range. **No shield.**

Since no shield is used, prefer a two-handed weapon. Match required strength to strength.

### [ Sage, Bard, Priest ]

No weapon or armor restrictions from these skills. Defer to any other skills acquired. If these are the only skills, match required strength to strength.

### Example

# Chapter 2: Success Rolls

> Source: Sword World.txt lines 2174–2392

Success rolls are the core resolution system of Sword World RPG. When a character attempts an action with uncertain outcome, a success roll determines success or failure.

## Chapter 2 Index

- 2.2 Target Scores
- 2.3 Checks

- 2.4 Bonuses and Penalties
- 2.5 Difficulty Checks
  - 2.5.1 Difficulty
  - 2.5.2 How to Make a Difficulty Check
  - 2.5.3 Analogy of Success and Failure
- 2.6 Success Rolls Using Adventurer Level
- 2.7 Resistance Rolls

## 2.1 Baseline Scores

A **baseline score** represents a character's competence at an activity.

> **baseline score = relevant skill level + ability bonus**

When swinging a sword, the relevant skill is fighter level and the relevant ability bonus is dexterity. See Chapter 6 for which skill and ability apply to each action.

**If a character has no skill related to the action, the baseline score is 0** regardless of ability.

**Example.** Ducard Ⅱ, fighter 2 with dexterity bonus +2, has baseline score 2 + 2 = **4** when wielding a sword.

**Example.** Ducard Ⅱ tries to pick a lock. Neither fighter nor ranger grants lockpicking, so his baseline is **0** — his dexterity bonus does not apply.

## 2.2 Target Scores

The **target score** represents the difficulty of the action — the higher it is, the harder the action. The game master sets target scores (see Part 2: Game Master Section).

**Example.** Ducard Ⅱ attacks a goblin. The GM declares a target score of 10.

## 2.3 Checks

Roll 2D and add the baseline score; the sum is the **final score**.

> final score (baseline + 2D) ≥ target → **success**
> final score (baseline + 2D) < target → **failure**

**Example.** Ducard Ⅱ attacks (baseline 4) vs. target 10. He needs a 2D roll of 6+ to hit.

### 2.3.1 Automatic Success and Automatic Failure

- **Double sixes (2D = 12):** automatic success, regardless of scores.
- **Double ones (2D = 2):** automatic failure, regardless of scores.

These are **not** definitive successes/failures — they just indicate the action succeeded or failed, not the magnitude. Unless a specific rule elevates a roll to definitive success/failure, treat it as an ordinary outcome.

## 2.4 Bonuses and Penalties

Circumstantial modifiers apply to the baseline score (e.g., +2 vs. a prone foe, −4 for darkness). The GM declares them before the roll.

> final score {baseline + 2D ± (bonus / penalty)} ≥ target → success
> final score {baseline + 2D ± (bonus / penalty)} < target → failure

Double sixes still auto-succeed and double ones still auto-fail regardless of modifiers.

## 2.5 Difficulty Checks

Some checks — typically perception-style (finding traps, spotting hidden things) — should not reveal to the player whether the action succeeded. These use **difficulty checks**.

### 2.5.1 Difficulty

**Difficulty** is a GM-set score representing how hard the action is. Unlike a target score, it is not the number the player is aiming at directly.

### 2.5.2 How to Make a Difficulty Check

The GM secretly rolls 2D and adds it to the difficulty to form the hidden target score. The player rolls 2D + baseline (+ modifiers) for their final score. The GM compares silently.

> final score ≥ target (difficulty + 2D) → success
> final score < target (difficulty + 2D) → failure

The GM does not announce success/failure — they describe only what the character perceives. Example outcomes for a find-trap check:

1. Trap exists, check succeeds → "There is a trap at the door."
2. Trap exists, check fails → "You don't find any traps."
3. No trap, check succeeds → "You don't find any traps."
4. No trap, check fails → "You don't find any traps."

Outcome #2 is the dangerous case.

### 2.5.3 Analogy of Success and Failure

Players gauge confidence from their own 2D roll:

- Roll 10–11: likely success; finding nothing probably means the coast is clear.
- Roll 3–4: likely failure; finding nothing is unreliable.
- Double sixes / double ones make the outcome unambiguous (auto-success / auto-failure).

Treat your roll as the **character's confidence** in their attempt.

**Caution.** Double ones is an auto-failure, **not a definitive failure**. After rolling double ones on a find-trap check and hearing "you don't find any traps," the correct response is: *"I didn't find any, but I'm not confident — treat it as if I never checked."* It is **wrong** to interpret it as "definitely no traps."

## 2.6 Success Rolls Using Adventurer Level

Some actions — things anyone gains with adventuring experience — use **adventurer level** instead of a specific skill level:

> baseline = adventurer level + ability bonus

See 6.6: Common Adventurer Abilities for the list. Section 2.7 Resistance Rolls also uses adventurer level.

A character with adventurer level 0 (no adventurer skills) has baseline **0** for these checks, regardless of ability scores.

## 2.7 Resistance Rolls

Two frequent adventurer-level checks have special names:

- **Life force resistance roll:** baseline = adventurer level + life force bonus. Used against physical dangers (poison, etc.).
- **Mental power resistance roll:** baseline = adventurer level + mental power bonus. Used against magic, curses, and monster special abilities that are magical in nature — **all checks against magic use mental power resistance**, even if the magic inflicts physical damage.

These baselines are also called **life force resistance** and **mental power resistance**.

**Example 1.** Ducard Ⅱ (adv. level 2, life force bonus +3) has life force resistance 5. A ghoul's paralyzing poison has toxicity 10 (target score). He rolls 2D = 5; 5 + 5 = 10 → resists paralysis, barely.

**Example 2.** Ducard Ⅱ (adv. level 2, mental power bonus +2) has mental power resistance 4. A sleep-magic trap presents target score 17 — normally hopeless, but his 2D = 12 (double sixes) is an automatic success. He escapes.

### Filling Out the Character Sheet

On the sheet, next to the life force and mental power fields, the bonus column and a **resistance** column are provided. Pre-compute and write:

> baseline (resistance) = adventurer level + life force / mental power bonus

# Chapter 3: Combat Rounds

Sword World RPG frequently uses a time unit called **rounds**, which corresponds to 10 seconds of in-game time.

In situations where multiple characters act at the same time, such as in combat, it is necessary to determine sequentially what kind of results each character's actions produce. Then, once you have resolved one character's action, you must resolve the next character's and so on, in an efficient sequence.

To do this, we stipulate that in-game time be divided into rounds, and that a character may perform one action per round. By having every character perform one action per round, we represent them as acting pseudo-simultaneously.

The round system is used mostly for combat. This is because it is especially important in combat to give equal opportunity to those who participate.

With that in mind, let's focus on combat here, while explaining how the game is played round by round.

## 3.1 Action Announcements

### 3.1.1 Order of Action Announcements

Each round begins with an announcement of what your character intends to do.

In combat, there are basically two opposing groups. Action announcements are made by each group.

Suppose there are three characters, a1, a2, and a3 in group A and two characters, b1 and b2 in group B. In this case, the order of action announcements is A → B or B → A. Characters who belong to different groups, such as a1→b1→a2...... do not take turns to announce their actions.

Which of the two will make their action announcements first is determined based on intelligence. The group with the highest intelligence among all the characters (including monsters, etc.) participating in combat will announce their action later. If a2 is the one with the highest intelligence between groups A and B as mentioned, then the announcements are made in the order of B → A, regardless of the intelligence of the other characters. Those who announce their actions later—the ones with highest representative intelligence—will have a significant advantage because they can announce their own actions after hearing their opponents' announcements.

If the two groups are equal in intelligence (i.e., the highest intelligence in each group is the same), compare the second highest in each group. If those are also the same, then compare the third highest.

If a settlement cannot be reached, the game master gets to announce their group's actions first (NPCs and monsters) and then the players follow.

The order in which action announcements are made within the same group (e.g., a1, a2, a3 within group A) may be determined freely and can also be changed from round to round.

If there are three or more groups instead of two, the order of action announcements is determined in the same way. The groups with the higher intelligence can decide on their actions later than the others.

In an action announcement, you cannot declare something impossible. Casting a spell while running at full speed is prohibited by the rules. If there's an enemy in front of you that's attacking you, attacking another enemy several meters away is not possible, just by common sense. These impossible actions cannot be declared, as a matter of course.

### 3.1.2 Player Action Announcements

A player must be specific when announcing their action.

Instead of *attack with my weapon*, they should say *attack the rightmost goblin with my sword*.

Instead of *use magic*, they should say *cast a fireball spell*.

You must be specific in this way. If the game master asks a question, be sure to answer it clearly.

### 3.1.3 NPC and Monster Action Announcements

When NPCs, monsters, etc. announce their actions, it's done by the game master who manages them. Specific action announcements may not be made at this time.

For example, simply say, *I am about to open my big mouth*. They won't know if it's opening its mouth to bite, or perhaps even breathe fire.

You may only declare, *I'm preparing to cast a spell*, and not give out the specific name of the spell. This is the difference in position between the players and the game master. The game master, as the person who manages the game, must know the actions of all the characters and monsters that appear. However, the players should only act from their characters' point of view. So, if a character cannot predict something, the player cannot know it either. When you see it for the first time, you won't know how a monster you don't recognize will act, and when an enemy tries to cast a spell you don't recognize, you won't know what that spell does.

Of course, if a character has a wealth of knowledge, or if they're an advanced spellcaster, they might know such things. If it's something the character should know, the game master should tell you when they announce the action.

## 3.2 Action Order

Once action announcements are complete, it is time to resolve both friends' and foes' actions. Combat will then actually take place.

This is done in order of agility, friend and foe alike. Therefore, it is possible to have a mixture of friends and foes, as in a1 → b1 → a2....... and so on.

If, as a result of the action of someone with higher agility, someone with lower agility is unable to perform their intended action, it means that their action is no longer possible.

If two have exactly the same agility, their actions are considered to be simultaneous.

For convenience, they are resolved in order, but even if you're attacked by an enemy of the same agility and fall down, you can still perform your intended actions.

> **Example:** Ducard Ⅱ is fighting a goblin. He has an agility of 12. On the other hand, the goblin's agility is 13, better than Ducard Ⅱ's. Therefore, the goblin will act before Ducard Ⅱ.
>
> Each of their action announcements are *attack the opponent (with a weapon)*. The goblin cuts at him with its claws. Ducard Ⅱ then fails to evade it and fall down with a deep wound. Ducard Ⅱ cannot carry out his intended attack on the goblin.
>
> Suppose that Ducard Ⅱ has an agility of 13, which is equal to that of a goblin (let's call him ⅡDash from now on, since he is a bit faster). In this case, both attacks are considered to be carried out simultaneously. During the course of the game, each attack check is made in the order of goblin → ⅡDash (or vice versa). Otherwise, it would be difficult to progress the game.
>
> Suppose that, as in the first example, ⅡDash is still hit badly enough to fall down as a result of the goblin's attack check. However, ⅡDash's action order = agility is the same as the goblin's, so he would've been slashing at the goblin at the same time. In other words, ⅡDash can make an attack check against the goblin.

### 3.2.1 Delaying an Action

Characters (including NPCs and monsters) do not have to act based on their agility and can instead delay their actions.

For example, suppose you know that a fellow wizard can use magic to increase the attack power of your sword. But what if your agility is higher than theirs? The support magic will be applied after you swing your sword, and the magic will be wasted.

In such a case, a character may lower their action order to 0. In other words, they will act only at the very end of the round.

If you want to delay an action, you must mention it in the action announcement phase, along with the action you intend to take.

## 3.3 Action Canceling

A character may cancel and not perform their announced action. For example, if an enemy that you intended to attack falls down due to the attack of a fellow player, no further attack is necessary. If you fall down yourself early in the order, you will not be able to act.

Or, in the middle of a round, your companion may ask you to help out by giving money or goods.

In such a case, a character may cancel their intended action.

However, characters cannot switch their actions. Just because a companion has knocked down an enemy in front of you does not mean that you can attack a different enemy. You also cannot switch from magic to a weapon attack because you intended to cast a spell but were blocked from chanting halfway through.

The choice is basically this: either continue with the announced action or cancel it.

### 3.3.1 When It's Possible to Change Attack Targets

As mentioned earlier, a character has only two choices: carry out their action as is or cancel it. However, there is an exception to this principle.

It's when you're engaged with several enemies and some of them are trying to withdraw (flee).

In this case, an intended attack on an enemy that is about to withdraw can be changed to another target that you're engaged with.

If the enemy that's trying to withdraw has a higher agility than you, you must switch to a different target, because according to your action order, the enemy will have escaped. At this time, an attacker can choose any new target to switch to (from among those they're engaged with).

If your agility is higher than that of the enemy who is trying to withdraw, you can attack them before they escape. However, even at this time, it's still acceptable to switch attack targets. As we'll see later, if all the enemies you're engaged with attempt to withdraw (flee), even if they have high agility, you can still attack, and you will have a higher chance of hitting than usual.

## 3.4 Movement and Actions

1 round lasts 10 seconds. Therefore, a character can also move during this time. However, when movement is made, actions are restricted. You cannot wield weapons or cast spells while running at full speed.

Characters have three options for movement: **full movement**, **normal movement**, or **stand still**. Each has different distances that can be traveled and different restrictions on actions that can be taken.

Below are some guidelines on how movement works and which actions can be taken. For any other actions, please use common sense based on the time frame of 1 round = 10 seconds. Final decisions are to be made by the game master.

### 3.4.1 Actions You Can Take Regardless of Movement Method

The following actions can be taken at any time, regardless of the movement method. They do not prevent other actions at all.

#### Drop a Weapon, Etc. On the Ground

Weapons and shields can be dropped on the ground at any time, regardless of whether they're one-handed, two-handed, or both-handed. If you do drop a weapon, etc. consider it as if you never had it in the first place, then choose your action.

### 3.4.2 Actions You Can Take During Full Movement

Full movement means that a character runs without looking aside. Naturally, you can advance farther, but the restrictions on your actions will also be greater. At this time, attacks from the enemy are also much more likely to hit.

The distance you can go with full movement is **character's agility × 3 meters**. For monsters, their movement speed is set separately from agility, so they can move up to **movement speed × 3 meters**.

The following actions can be taken during full movement:

#### Ready a Sheathed Weapon

You can wield up to two, one in each hand. If you want to switch your weapon from a projectile such as a bow, to a melee weapon, you can drop your previous weapon on the ground, and then you can ready a new weapon. This is because, from the beginning, it's assumed your hands were empty.

However, this is limited to items that are already worn and ready to go, such as those kept in a sheath. You cannot pick up a weapon that was left on the ground. If you're carrying a shield on your back, you can also ready it.

#### Switch a Weapon From Hand to Hand

You can move something in your right hand to your left hand, or hold something you had in one hand with both hands.

Some weapons can be held in either one or both hands. If you have such a weapon in one hand and a shield in the other, if you drop the shield, the weapon is now considered to be in only one hand, so you can then hold it with both hands.

#### Sheath a Weapon, Etc.

You can sheath up to two, one in each hand. However, this is only possible if there is a sheath, etc. You can also place a shield on your back.

#### Withdraw (Flee) From Engagement

You can escape from an opponent within melee attack range.

If an attack is declared on a character who is withdrawing, another character is needed to suffer the enemy's attack in their place (defend against the attack).

If you do not have a companion to take on your opponent, you will escape with your back completely turned, and you will suffer a **-4 penalty** when evading the attack. Also, in that case, regardless of your character's agility, they will only escape at the end of the round (agility 0, based on action order). In other words, there is always a chance for the enemy to attack a character before they withdraw.

### 3.4.3 Actions You Can Take During Normal Movement

This is movement while staying alert to your surroundings.

The distance you can move is a number of meters equal to your character's agility (or a monster's movement speed).

At this time, the following actions are possible:

#### Ready a Weapon

As during full movement, you can ready a weapon from a sheath, etc. You can wield up to two, one in each hand.

#### Sheath a Weapon, Etc.

The same as during full movement.

#### Pick a Weapon Up off the Ground and Ready It

If a weapon or shield has fallen on the ground, you can pick it up and ready it. However, that weapon cannot be used to attack this round.

#### Switch Out a One-Handed Weapon

Return a weapon in your hand to its sheath, etc. and ready a different weapon. Only one can be switched out. For example, if you have a sword and a shield, you cannot return them both and ready a different weapon.

If you do have a sword and a shield, you can drop one of them on the ground, return the other one, then ready a different weapon. If you drop one of the things you were holding, it's as if you were only holding one from the beginning.

#### Ready a Weapon and Attack

Take a weapon out of a sheath, etc. that you're wearing and use it to attack immediately. However, bows and other projectiles cannot be used to attack while moving.

#### Switch a Weapon's Hands and Attack

Switch a weapon you're using with only one hand to both hands and attack. This action may be effective if you have a weapon that can be used with one or both hands.

If you're holding a weapon in one hand and a shield in the other, and that weapon can be used with both hands, you can drop the shield on the ground and attack with both hands, since it's as if you were holding the weapon in only one hand from the beginning.

#### Attack with a Readied Weapon

If you already have a weapon ready, you can of course attack with that weapon. However, bows and other projectiles cannot be used to attack while moving.

### 3.4.4 Actions You Can Take While Standing Still

Although stationary, you do not have to remain completely still; you can move up to 3 meters.

In this state, all actions you can take during normal movement described in the previous section can be performed.

#### Use Magic and Projectiles

Above all, the advantage of standing still is the ability to use magic and attack with projectiles. Conversely, if you are not standing still, you cannot use magic or projectiles.

If you want to attack with a projectile, please follow the process of *actions you can take during normal movement*. If you chose stand still, then *attack with that weapon* can be read as *attack with that projectile*. However, when you're engaged (being attacked by a melee weapon), you cannot use projectiles.

If you choose to stand still, in addition to magic and projectiles, you can also take the following special action:

#### Stand Up and Ready Yourself

From a state of falling down, stand up and ready yourself. You can only ready one weapon, etc. that you're wearing. You cannot pick up any items on the ground.

# Chapter 4: Weapon Combat

Combat is a major element of fantasy RPGs. It's common practice for thugs to attack those they encounter without reason, but you'll be in trouble if you don't know how to sweep away the falling sparks. This chapter explains the rules of combat with weapons.

## 4.1 Weapon and Armor Restrictions

A very frail man, all skin and bones, dressed in heavy plate armor and wielding a long greatsword.......

All joking aside, however, if we want to be realistic, weapons and armor used should be limited by a character's strength. All weapons and armor have a **required strength** to use them. Characters may not use weapons or armor with a required strength greater than their own strength.

A character's arms are largely divided into weapons, shields, and armor. Required strength should not be added up, but rather considered separately for each of these. In other words, a character with 14 strength can wield a weapon with a required strength of 14, wield a shield with a required strength of 14, and wear armor with a required strength of 14.

Under the basic rules, a character can be armed with either *one-handed weapon + shield + armor* or *two-handed weapon + armor*. Of course, *one-handed weapon + armor*, *two-handed weapon without armor*, etc. are also possible, and it is acceptable to carry a spare weapon on your waist or back, as long as it is within the bounds of common sense. However, you may not hold two weapons in both hands or hold a shield in each hand—it is fine to do so, but a shield in your dominant hand or a weapon in your non-dominant hand is of no use.

## 4.2 Weapon Combat Skills

To be good at weapon combat, a character must have skills. The skills that can be used for this purpose are limited to the following three:

Fighter skill, thief skill, and ranger skill.

**Fighter Skill.** The fighter skill is the most basic skill for weapon combat. In the following explanation, we will focus on this skill.

**Thief Skill.** A thief also has the ability to fight, but only with light equipment. Specifically, you can only fight if the weapon or armor you're using requires up to half (rounded up) of your character's strength. As for armor, it's limited not only by required strength but also by type. Metal armor other than ring mail may not be used, even if the required strength (half of strength) is met.

**Ranger Skill.** The ranger skill is proficient with projectiles, and only when using a weapon that is designated as such—throwable, made for throwing, or shooting on Table 1-8: Weapons—can they fight as well as a fighter when attacking. There is no ability to attack with melee weapons. Weapons that can be used for both melee and throwing, as indicated by *throwable* on the Weapons table, are useful only when used for throwing, not for melee.

The ranger skill cannot be used for defense.

## 4.3 Projectile Restrictions

There are some restrictions when handling projectiles such as bows and javelins.

One is their range. There is no bow that has infinite reach. The maximum of shooting weapons such as bows and arrows—those marked *shooting* on the Weapons table—is **100m**, and of throwing weapons such as spears—those marked *throwable* or *made for throwing* on the Weapons table—is **20m**. If an enemy is beyond this distance, the projectile will not hit them.

In addition, projectiles cannot be used in melee. When there's an enemy in front of you, you can never use a projectile marked *shooting* or *made for throwing*. Weapons marked as *throwable* can also be used as melee weapons, but not as projectiles.

Again, movement is also restricted when using projectiles. A character must choose **stand still** as their movement method, and can only move up to 3 meters in 1 round.

## 4.4 Scores Used in Weapon Combat

During weapon combat, each of the following scores are used:

Attack power, strike power, bonus damage, critical target, evasion speed, defense power, and damage reduction.

**Attack Power.** This is the number needed to determine whether a character's attack will hit or not. The higher this score, the higher the probability that a character's attack will hit.

Attack power is calculated with the following formula:

> attack power = fighter (thief／ranger) skill level + dexterity bonus

If fighter (or thief, ranger) skill level is 0, then attack power is 0 regardless of the character's dexterity bonus.

**Strike Power.** This is the number needed to determine how much damage a character's attack will deal if it hits. The greater this score, the more damage a character will deal.

Strike power is the same number as the required strength of the weapon used. However, if the weapon is a two-handed weapon, it's instead required strength + 5.

> strike power = required strength of weapon (+5 if a two-handed weapon)

If a character does not have a weapon (they're barehanded), strike power is 0.

**Bonus Damage.** Characters with deep experience in combat can handle weapons effectively. In addition, characters with high strength can wield weapons more powerfully. These characters can deal more damage to their foes, even when using the same sized weapon. This is represented by bonus damage.

Bonus damage is calculated with the following formula:

> bonus damage = fighter (thief／ranger) skill level + strength bonus

If fighter (or thief, ranger) skill level is 0, bonus damage is 0 regardless of the character's strength bonus.

**Critical Target.** If a weapon hits, it'll sometimes deal more damage than normal. For example, when you successfully exploit a gap in your opponent's armor, or when you successfully damage an opponent's vital point, such as an eye.

In Sword World RPG, this is resolved with a process called a **critical**. The critical target is the number needed to determine whether a critical occurs.

Usually the critical target is **10**. However, when attacking with the thief skill, it is instead **9**.

The critical target is a *lower is better* score, which is rare in Sword World RPG. Therefore, an attack using the thief skill is more likely to result in a critical than an attack using the regular fighter skill.

> critical target = 10 (9 if using thief skill)

**Evasion Speed.** In combat, characters are not the only ones who can attack. Naturally, the enemy will also attack the characters. To evade an enemy's attack, a score called evasion speed is necessary.

A character with excellent evasion speed will be able to evade enemy attacks more easily. No matter how formidable the weapon, it's not that scary if it doesn't hit.

> evasion speed = fighter (thief) skill level + agility bonus

If fighter (or thief) skill level is 0, then evasion speed is 0 regardless of the character's agility bonus.

A character holding a shield receives a **+1 bonus** to evasion speed. The size of the shield (whether it is a small or large shield) is completely irrelevant unless you introduce the optional rule 16.2: Weapon and Armor Modifiers.

A fighter (or thief) skill level of 0 does not prevent you from receiving this shield bonus. Therefore, the evasion speed of such a character will be 1.

**Defense Power.** If by a stroke of bad luck, an opponent's attack hits, a character can still reduce the damage by means of armor. It is defense power that represents this.

Defense power is the same number as the required strength of the armor worn by the character.

> defense power = required strength of armor

If a character is not wearing any armor (just clothes or nothing at all), then defense power is 0.

**Damage Reduction.** Those who are called heroes do not die from just a few injuries. In Sword World RPG, a character with a high adventurer level, that is, a character who has a high level in any adventurer skill, is considered a hero. Therefore, we determine a hero's resistance to death by their adventurer level.

Damage reduction is a score that reduces the damage a character would have taken. It's exactly equal to a character's adventurer level.

> damage reduction = adventurer level

#### Filling out the Character Sheet

Please write these scores in the designated fields on the Character Sheet. This will make the game run more smoothly.

The entry fields may seem complicated, but this is to assist you when using Chapter 16: Optional Rules for Combat. Now, don't worry about it too much. It is sufficient to write down each weapon, shield, and armor along with their required strength, as well as attack power, strike power, critical target, bonus damage, evasion speed, defense power, and damage reduction in the total field at the bottom.

Have you learned each of these terms? Now, let's get into the actual description of the combat procedure. Combat consists of two checks: one is made to determine if an attack hits, and if the total result is a hit, another is made to determine the damage dealt by the attack.

These procedures differ when the attacker and defender are character against monster, monster against character, or character against character. There is one other case, monster against monster, but since only the game master should know how to handle this case, we won't explain it here.

## 4.5 Attacks From Characters Against Monsters

### 4.5.1 Hit Checks

When a character attacks a monster, they must make a success roll **hit check**, using attack power as the baseline score.

In other words, you roll 2D and add the result to your attack power (fighter/thief/ranger skill level + dexterity bonus) in order to meet or exceed a certain target score.

The target score at this time varies depending on the monster. In the monsters' data, there is a score called **evasion points**, which becomes the target score.

> character's attack power + 2D ≥ monster's evasion points → attack hits
> character's attack power + 2D < monster's evasion points → attack fails

※Double sixes will automatically hit, and double ones will automatically fail.

Since a hit check is also a type of success roll, double ones is an automatic failure and double sixes is an automatic success.

> **Example:** Ducard Ⅱ is fighting a goblin. His attack power is 4 (fighter skill level 2, dexterity bonus 2) and the goblin's evasion points are 10.
>
> His player rolls the dice to make a hit check. The roll is 8. 4+8=12, which is more than 10. The attack hit the target perfectly.

>
> If the third roll were a 2 (double ones), the base damage would instead be 6+8+0=14.

### 4.5.3 Bonus Damage

Once you've determined base damage, you must then add your character's bonus damage. This will be the total amount of damage the character deals the monster.

> **Example:** Ducard Ⅱ's bonus damage is 4 (fighter skill level 2, strength bonus 2).
>
> If base damage is 4, the total damage is 4+4=8,
> If base damage is 18, the total damage is 18+4=22,
> If base damage is 14, the total damage is 14+4=18.

When determining base damage (strike roll), if you suddenly roll double ones, the damage will always be 0. No matter how much bonus damage you have, it means nothing.

### 4.5.4 Monster Defense Points

Monsters can repel and hold off attacks with their thick skins and hard scales. In monster data, this is represented by **defense points**. This indicates the amount of damage a monster can reduce. Please note that a monster's defense points are treated differently from a character's defense power.

> **Example:** A goblin has 4 defense points. The goblin can prevent 4 points of damage with its skin.

### 4.5.5 Final Damage

The total amount of damage a character deals (base damage + bonus damage), minus the monster's defense points, equals the final damage they're able to deal to the monster.

> final damage = base damage + bonus damage − monster's defense points

> **Example:** If the base damage dealt by Ducard Ⅱ is 4, 18, or 14, respectively, the final damage he could deal to the goblin would be:
>
>  4 (base damage) + 4 (bonus damage) − 4 (goblin's defense points) = 4
> 18 (base damage) + 4 (bonus damage) − 4 (goblin's defense points) = 18
> 14 (base damage) + 4 (bonus damage) − 4 (goblin's defense points) = 14

If final damage is 0 or negative, it means that no damage was dealt. The attack was prevented by thick skin or hard scales.

## 4.6 Attacks From Monsters Against Characters

### 4.6.1 Hit (Evasion) Checks

When a monster attacks a character, whether or not the attack hits is determined from the perspective of *did the character dodge the monster's attack?*.

This check is also made with a success roll.

The baseline score is the character's evasion speed. The target score is still different for each monster, but it's called the monster's **attack points**.

> evasion speed + 2D ≥ monster's attack points → evasion success (monster's attack fails)
> evasion speed + 2D < monster's attack points → evasion failure (monster's attack hits)

※Double sixes will automatically evade, and double ones will automatically fail.

Since this is a success roll, double sixes and double ones are automatic successes and automatic failures, respectively.

> **Example:** Ducard Ⅱ has an evasion speed of 5 (fighter level 2, agility bonus +2, has a shield). On the other hand, the goblin has 10 attack points.
>
> The player rolls the dice to determine if they evade the goblin's attack. The roll is 4. 5+4=9, which is short of the target score (the goblin's attack points) of 10. Ducard Ⅱ is attacked by the goblin.

### 4.6.2 Damage Dealt by Monsters

If a monster's attack hits (or if a character fails to evade), you must look for the base damage dealt by the monster.

This is determined for each monster by the monster's **strike points**. Strike points are fixed and do not change.

> **Example:** A goblin has 7 strike points.

### 4.6.3 Defense Rolls

Characters can reduce the damage they receive from monsters by means of armor. You can determine the amount of damage reduction by using Table 4-1: Rating Table. On the Rating Table, look at the column where the key number equals your character's defense power. ### 4.6.4 Damage Reduction

Characters can also reduce damage using their adventurer level. The result of your defense roll plus this damage reduction equals the final amount of damage your character can reduce.

However, if the result of your defense roll is double ones, your character's damage reduction is meaningless. No matter how high your character's damage reduction (adventurer level), that damage cannot be reduced at all.

### 4.6.5 Final Damage

The final damage your character suffers from a monster equals the monster's strike points minus the result of your defense roll plus your character's damage reduction.

> final damage = monster's strike points − (defense roll result + damage reduction)

> **Example:** If the result of Ducard Ⅱ's defense roll is 0 (roll 3) or 6 (roll 12), respectively, the final damage he will suffer is calculated to be:
>
> 7 (goblin's strike points) − 0 (defense roll) + 2 (damage reduction) = 5
> 7 (goblin's strike points) − 6 (defense roll) + 2 (damage reduction) = −1
>
> If the defense roll came up double ones, no such calculation would be made, and the goblin's 7 strike points would be the damage.

If final damage is 0 or negative, that means you took no damage at all. The monster's attack was completely blocked by your armor.

## 4.7 Attacks From Characters Against Characters

While infighting between player characters is something you should avoid at all costs, fights against non-player characters are common. Sometimes, especially when the mastermind behind an incident is a dark priest, you'll have to fight against someone whose data was created for a character.

In a fight between these characters, proceed as follows:

### 4.7.1 Hit Checks

To make a hit check, both sides must roll the dice (2D). The attacker adds their roll to their attack power, and the defender adds their roll to their evasion speed.

> attacker's final score = attack power + 2D
> defender's final score = evasion speed + 2D

If the attacker's final score exceeds that of the defender, the attack hits. If there is a tie or the defender's is greater, the attack fails.

> attacker's attack power + 2D > defender's evasion speed + 2D → attack hits
> attacker's attack power + 2D ≤ defender's evasion speed + 2D → attack fails

> **Example:** Ducard Ⅱ suddenly found himself in a dispute with another adventurer, Bucky. Ducard Ⅱ has higher agility, so he attacks first. Ducard Ⅱ's attack power is 4, while Bucky's evasion speed is 5.
>
> Ducard Ⅱ rolls a 7, and Bucky rolls a 6.
>
> Ducard Ⅱ's final score is 4+7=11, while Bucky's final score is 5+6=11. It's a tie.
>
> In combat between characters, a tie is considered an attack failure. Ducard Ⅱ's attack was narrowly dodged.

#### Double Sixes and Double Ones

If a roll comes up double sixes or double ones during combat between characters, the situation is resolved as follows:

- If the attacker's roll is double ones, the attack fails without question. The defender doesn't even have to roll the dice.
- If the attacker's roll is double sixes, it's basically an automatic hit. However, if the defender's roll is also double sixes, the attack fails.
- If the defender's roll is double ones, it's basically an automatic hit. However, if the attacker's roll is also double ones, the attack fails.
- If the defender's roll is double sixes, the attack will always fail. It doesn't matter even if the attacker rolls double sixes.

### 4.7.2 Damage Checks

If your attack hits, you must then determine damage. This procedure is exactly the same for characters as it was during combat with monsters.

The attacker must make a strike roll to determine their base damage, then add their bonus damage.

The defender must make a defense roll, then add their damage reduction to the result to determine how much damage they reduced.

The difference is the damage dealt to the defender.

> **Example:** Let's go back to the fight between Ducard Ⅱ and Bucky. Bucky dodges Ducard Ⅱ's attack, then counterattacks. As a result of his hit check, it's a hit.
>
> Bucky then determines the damage he deals. Bucky is wielding a battle-ax (required strength 15) with both hands, so he has a strike power of 20. The result of his strike roll is 11 (base damage 9). That's a critical. His following 2D roll is 9 (base damage 7). His bonus damage is 5 (fighter skill level 3, strength bonus +2), which brings the total amount of damage to 9+7+5=21.
>
> Ducard Ⅱ now determines how much damage his armor prevents. His defense roll result is 7, and his armor's defense power is 7, so the damage that'll be reduced by his armor is 3. After adding his damage reduction of 2, 3+2=5 points is the final amount of damage Ducard Ⅱ is able to reduce.
>
> As a result, the damage that Ducard Ⅱ suffers is 21−5=16 points.

## 4.8 Applying Damage

Damage reduces a character's life force (or a monster's life points). This reduction is temporary and can be recovered naturally or by magic.

After damage is applied, a character (monster) can still act if they have at least 1 life force (point) remaining.

If you suffer damage and your life force (points) is reduced, then suffer more damage, your life force (points) will be further reduced from there.

> **Example:** Ducard Ⅱ has 18 life force. Ducard Ⅱ is hit by Bucky's attack and suffers 16 points of damage, reducing his life force to 2 points. It's still possible for him to act.

## 4.9 Unconscious and Death Checks

If, as a result of damage (accumulated damage), a character's (monster's) life force (points) becomes 0 or negative, that character (monster) will fall unconscious. It is no longer possible for them to act. Also, that character (monster) is now in danger of dying.

> **Example:** Ducard Ⅱ, with 2 points of life force left, was hit by Bucky's ax again in the next round, suffering 6 points of damage. As a result, Ducard Ⅱ now has a life force of −4. Ducard Ⅱ faints and collapses.

### 4.9.1 Adventurer Death Checks

Any adventurer who falls to 0 or negative life force is now at risk of dying. You must roll the dice to determine if your character survives.

This check is made using a life force resistance roll. The baseline score is **life force resistance** (adventurer level + life force bonus) and the target score is **7**. If the character's life force is negative, the negative amount will be used as a penalty.

> life force resistance + 2D − (damage applied beyond life force) ≥ 7 → survival
> life force resistance + 2D − (damage applied beyond life force) < 7 → death

> **Example:** Ducard Ⅱ, with a life force of −4, must make a death check. His life force resistance is 5 (adventurer level 2, life force bonus +3). Since Ducard Ⅱ has a life force of −4, that will also be the penalty. His result after rolling 2D is 7.
>
> 5 (life force resistance) + 7 (roll) − 4 (penalty) = 8, which is greater than 7. Ducard Ⅱ survives. If the result of this death check had been 5 or lower, Ducard Ⅱ would have met an untimely end.

### 4.9.2 Death Checks Every Hour

Characters with 0 or negative life force remain unconscious, even if they successfully survived a death check. If they're given treatment by magic, etc. and have their life force increased by at least 1 (positive) point, they will regain consciousness, but if they're left unconscious, they may die.

Characters with 0 or negative life force must make a death check every hour. The target score is still 7, and if their life force is still negative, it's still a penalty.

If the result is double sixes (automatic success), the character will regain consciousness on their own. At this time, their life force becomes 1 point.

If the result is any success other than double sixes, the character is alive but remains unconscious. In one hour, they must make another death check.

If the result is failure, the character will die.

### 4.9.3 Monster Death Check

Monsters with 0 or negative life points must also make a check to see if they live or die. Do not roll the dice at this time. A score called the monster's **life point resistance** is used instead. Subtract the amount of negative life points, if any, from life point resistance, and if the result is 7 or greater, the monster is alive. If the result is 6 or lower, the monster is dead.

> **Example:** A goblin, Scarnose, was hit by an adventurer's sword and fell down with −6 life points. A goblin's life point resistance score is 10. 10−6=4, which is less than 7. Scarnose draws his last breath.

A monster's first death check determines whether or not it survives. If it's alive after a death check, it recovers 1 life point every hour, and will regain consciousness when it reaches (positive) 1 life point.

### 4.9.4 Mercy

If a character does not want to kill their opponent, they can declare **mercy**. However, to do so, their weapon must be a dull weapon (a bludgeoning weapon). On Table 1-8: Weapons, maces, clubs, and staves are classified as dull weapons. However, even if an item is classified as one of these, if you throw it, you cannot declare mercy. You can also declare mercy when using a barehanded attack. You must declare mercy before you make a hit check for your attack. It cannot be done after a hit or after damage check.

A character who falls down with 0 or negative life force due to an attack that was declared to be mercy, will only fail their death check if double ones are rolled. On any other roll, the character remains alive.

If left untreated, they must make another death check after 1 hour, and again, will only die on double ones. If successful, then even if it's not double sixes, they will regain consciousness with 1 point of life force. If they fail, then unless they roll double ones, they will continue to remain unconscious.

Whenever an adventurer declares that their attack on a monster is a mercy, that monster will always remain alive. If left alone, it will always regain consciousness.

## 4.10 Excellent Weapons and Armor

A variety of weapons and armor can be found in Forcelia. Some are of high quality made by master craftsmen, some have magical powers, and others are made of unusual materials such as silver or mithril silver.

When a character obtains and uses these weapons and armor, their various scores are modified as follows.

### 4.10.1 High-Quality Weapons and Armor

There are people in the world who are called master craftsmen, and they sometimes create weapons and armor that are better than normal. These high-quality weapons and armor are available to adventurers, if they seek them out.

High-quality weapons and armor are easier to handle, and are made with lower required strength than usual. For example, a broadsword that originally had a required strength 16 could be made with required strength 15. As a result, this broadsword can be wielded by someone with 15 strength, but still has a strike power of 16.

It is important to note that such weapons and armor cannot exceed the limits of the weapon or armor itself. For example, if you want a high-quality broadsword, its required strength (size) is naturally limited to 8–16. Since there is no such broadsword with a natural required strength of 17, that means there is no such thing as a broadsword with required strength 16 and strike power 17.

Also, even if you search all over Alecrast, you will usually only find one with a required strength up to 5 lower than usual. You will not see any with a required strength that's 6 lower than usual.

So long as required strength is up to 5 lower than usual, a character can purchase one at a store. However, if you want one of higher quality, you must go to a larger town. For reference, a guideline is printed here (Table 4-5: Guideline for Obtaining High-Quality Weapons and Armor).

Table 4-5: Guideline for Obtaining High-Quality Weapons and Armor

| Required Strength Reduction | Where to Find                                          |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| 5 less                      | Royal city level in a large country                    |
| 4 less                      | Large city in a large country, royal city level in a small country |
| 3 less                      | Reasonable city                                        |
| 2 less                      | Town of about 1000 people                              |
| 1 less                      | All but the most remote village                        |

These high-quality weapons and armor can be expensive. Their price increases by 20% for every 1 required strength lower than usual. 2 lower will be 40% more expensive, 3 lower will be 60% more expensive, 4 lower will be 80% more expensive, and 5 lower will be twice the original price.

In addition, no matter how high quality it is, nothing can affect its attack power or evasion speed. It'll also have no effect on monsters that can only be wounded by magic or magical weapons (such as spirits, beastmen, etc.).

### 4.10.2 Silver Weapons and Armor

Silver is a special metal, imbued with magical powers. It is also one of only two types of metal that's known to exist in the spirit world.

Even silver weapons and armor have no differences in their strike power, defense power, attack power, or evasion speed at all.

However, silver weapons, due to their magic power, can deal damage to monsters that can only be wounded by magic or magical weapons.

Also, shamans are not normally able to wear metal armor (as metal armor prevents them from using spirit magic), but they can use spirit magic while wearing it, if it's silver armor.

To buy silver weapons and armor in a store, you must be in a town about the size of the royal city level in a small country. Silver weapons and armor will also cost 5 times the original price. You can also ask for a high-quality item of silver, but the price will be even higher. Just think in terms of multiplication. For example, the price of a silver item with a required strength 5 times lower than the original would be 5 × 2 = 10 times the price of the original.

### 4.10.3 Magical Weapons and Armor

In Forcelia, there are magical weapons and armor that were once created during the age of the magical kingdom (Kastuul). These magical weapons and armor are seldom found, and are usually rarely traded.

Characters may obtain these magical weapons and armor through their adventures. The power of magical weapons and armor is represented by a +1 or +2 score.

In Forcelia, the limit for this power is +3. There are only a scant few weapons and armor in the world with +3 power, and any with +4 or more have yet to be found.

**Magical Weapons.** Magical weapons have exceptional attack power and bonus damage. If you use a +1 magical weapon, you receive a +1 bonus to attack power and bonus damage. If +2, those bonuses are +2, and if +3, they're +3.

Magical weapons can deal damage to monsters that normal weapons cannot harm.

**Magical Shields.** Magical shields increase evasion speed. A +1 magic shield grants +1 evasion speed, +2 if +2. If +3, it is +3.

**Magical Armor.** Magical armor increases evasion speed and damage reduction. If you wear +1 magical armor, you gain +1 to evasion speed and damage reduction. +2 means +2, and +3 means +3. The damage reduction bonus from magical armor applies only to weapon attacks. It does not reduce damage caused by magic.

Even if it is magical, if it is metal armor, a shaman cannot wear it.

Magical weapons and armor are not usually traded, but if you are very lucky you may be able to buy them if they're +1. A guide to prices can be found in Chapter 14: Treasure. Also, they offer basically no bonus to strike power or defense power, same as required strength.

High-quality ones (made with lower required strength) seem to exist only rarely.

### 4.10.4 Mithril Silver Weapons and Armor

Mithril silver, also known as **true silver**, is produced in very few places in the world. Mithril silver is itself a mineral imbued with magic, and when processed, it gains power. However, the art of forging mithril silver was lost with the fall of the magical kingdom, so it has not been handed down to the present day.

If a character ever sees them, mithril silver weapons and armor are definitely a legacy from the era of the magical kingdom.

Mithril silver weapons and armor are always magical items and, like magical weapons and armor, have a power of +1 or +2. This power affects attack power, evasion speed, bonus damage, and damage reduction. The maximum power is +3. No one has yet discovered anything with a power of +4 or higher.

Mithril silver is also a very strong metal. For this reason, strike power and defense power are calculated by adding **+5** to required strength.

What makes them different from high-quality weapons and armor is that they do not lower required strength but rather increase strike power and defense power. Therefore, you can find a broadsword clad in mithril silver that has required strength 16 and strike power 21.

Mithril silver and high-quality—such as a broadsword made of mithril silver, originally with required strength 16 but made with 5 less, and also with +5 strike power because it's mithril silver, resulting in required strength 11 and strike power 21—is almost always rare.

Mithril silver weapons can deal damage to monsters that normal weapons cannot harm.

Mithril silver, like regular silver, is a metal that exists in the spirit world. Therefore, a shaman can wear mithril silver armor and still use spirit magic.

Chain mail made of mithril silver does not inhibit bodily movement as much as that made of steel (but splint armor, lamellar armor, and plate armor still do). Mithril silver chain mail does not interfere with the thief or ranger skills.

Mithril silver weapons and armor are never, if ever, placed in shops or traded for money.

It should be noted that, although this does not affect the numeric aspects of the game, mithril silver can be made to emit a variety of colors depending on how it's processed, and these colors will never fade.

Dwarves in particular excelled in their craft, and powerful and beautiful mithril silver items made by dwarves can still be found in Alecrast even today.

## 4.11 Damage Recovery

Damage dealt to a character's life force can be recovered in a variety of ways.

### 4.11.1 Magical Recovery

Life force that a character has lost due to damage is relatively easy to recover through the use of magic or items with magical powers.

The degree of this recovery can be found in the descriptions of each spell or magic item.

### 4.11.2 First-Aid

A character with 0 or negative life force will recover up to 1 (positive) point if given treatment by someone with the ranger skill, healer skill, or similar.

This treatment takes 30 minutes. It can also instantly recover life force to 1 point, no matter how great the negative, but it cannot recover life force to 2 or more points.

### 4.11.3 Natural Recovery

If a character is conscious and at rest (full rest in a safe place), that character recovers 1 point of life force per day.

If someone with the healer skill is nursing them, that character can recover even more life force each day. For more information, see 7.4: Healer Skill.

If a character is not at rest (e.g., traveling, fighting monsters in a labyrinth, not getting much sleep at night due to keeping watch), that character cannot recover any life force at all.

# Chapter 6: Adventurer Skills Other Than Combat and Magic

*(Source txt lines 7343–8242)*

This chapter explains how adventurer skills other than fighter and rune masters — namely the thief, ranger, sage, and bard skills — can be useful in your adventures. An adventurer can only become a worthy adventurer if they possess these abilities. Those with only a high fighter skill level may become excellent mercenaries, but they are third-rate adventurers.

## 6.1 Precautions When Using Skills

The abilities associated with these skills, including the thief skill, are mostly determined by success rolls.

If the rules for determining the target score or difficulty are clearly indicated, the game master should follow those rules, otherwise the game master must follow the rules under **9.1: Setting Target Scores and Difficulties**, and decide on the target score or difficulty of the action.

Some abilities say **not retryable**. Once you fail such an ability, you cannot attempt it again on the same target. For example, the thief skill's lockpicking ability cannot be retried. If a thief attempts to pick a lock and fails, no further attempts can be made to pick that lock (and any attempts will always fail).

These can be attempted again if the baseline score for a success roll increases (that is, if the skill level increases or the ability bonus of the relevant ability score increases).

## 6.2 Thief Skill

The owner of this skill is called a **thief**. However, not all of them make their living by stealing from others. The thief skill includes abilities necessary for theft such as pickpocketing and lockpicking, but if anything they're specialized craftsmen familiar with the techniques needed behind the scenes of society. They may be hired by the king for espionage, etc., and depending on the money they may undertake assassinations.

The thief skill is taught at the **thieves' guild**. People who aren't in the guild usually can't learn it. Each year, guild members must give 10% of their annual income or 500 silver coins (whichever is higher) to their guild. Even those who have not joined a guild can directly learn the thief skill from those who have a higher thief skill level than themselves, but if the guild finds out that such outsider thieves (including those who have not paid the prescribed amount) used this skill to do business, they'll send an assassin. It is also illegal to use this skill to commit theft, and if caught, punishment awaits.

Some abilities cannot be used unless you're wearing light equipment. If you want to make full use of your thief skill, the required strength of your equipment (weapons, shields, armor) must only be up to half your strength (rounded up). In particular, armor must adhere to this restriction and must also be ring mail or non-metal. Abilities subject to this restriction are marked **(Equipment restriction)**; otherwise **(No equipment restriction)**.

**Abilities:** ① Tumble, ② Lockpicking, ③ Acrobatics, ④ Mnemonics, ⑤ Listen, ⑥ Stealthy Step, ⑦ Pickpocket, ⑧ Hide, ⑨ Search, ⑩ Climb, ⑪ Special Combat, ⑫ Long Jump／High Jump, ⑬ Follow, ⑭ Disguise, ⑮ Treasure Appraisal, ⑯ Find／Sense Trap, ⑰ Remove／Set Trap.

### ① Tumble [Agility] (Equipment restriction)
Even if a thief falls from a high place, they can reduce the damage by being skillful at taking a defensive tumble. See ④ Climb under **6.6: Common Adventurer Abilities**.

### ② Lockpicking [Dexterity] (No equipment restriction, not retryable)
The ability to open locks on doors, treasure chests, etc. The thieves' guild sells **thieves' tools** to guild members for 100 silver coins. Even without these tools, you can try using wire, etc., but the probability of success will be lower (impose a **-2** modifier on success rolls). The game master sets the lock's target score depending on the difficulty. You can attempt to pick a lock normally even if wearing armor that exceeds the restriction, but this is almost impossible when wearing metal gauntlets, etc. (use common sense).

### ③ Acrobatics [Agility] (Equipment restriction)
Thieves are skilled at activities that require physical balance and agility, such as tightrope walking and jumping from branch to branch. Each time a character performs such an action, the game master must set a target score and ask for a success roll check.

### ④ Mnemonics [Intelligence] (No equipment restriction, not retryable)
The ability to remember what you witness. Even if a book is written in a language you don't know, you can memorize the shape of each letter exactly as it is, and write it down later. A success roll is made when **recalling**, not when memorizing. The game master determines the target score based on the contents being recalled and the time since you witnessed it. Add a **+2** modifier when you try to recall something you've already specifically tried to consciously memorize.

### ⑤ Listen [Intelligence] (Equipment restriction)
Used to hear certain sounds from behind walls, doors, or from a distance. You can also choose and listen to a specific sound from a mixture of sounds. If you are wearing metal armor or heavy armor, the noise it makes will interfere with your hearing. A helmet will clearly interfere with this ability.

### ⑥ Stealthy Step [Agility] (Equipment restriction)
The ability to move without making a sound. The thief makes a success roll using **thief skill + dexterity bonus** as the baseline score, and the person trying to hear the sound makes one using **adventurer level + intelligence bonus** as the baseline score. If the thief's final score is greater, the sound will never be heard. On a tie or if the listener's is greater, the thief is overheard. If they're not paying attention to the sound, impose a **-4** penalty on the listener. If the listener also has the thief skill, they may use **thief skill level + intelligence bonus** (the listen ability above). If they can hear, they discern not only presence but also its general appearance (walking speed, stride length, number of people, etc.).

### ⑦ Pickpocket [Dexterity] (Equipment restriction)
The ability to see an opening on an opponent and pickpocket their wallet, small items, etc. The thief rolls with **thief skill level + dexterity bonus**, the victim with **adventurer level + intelligence bonus**. Success if the thief's final score is greater; failure on a tie or if the victim's is greater. On failure, it will definitely be exposed. The game master may add modifiers depending on surroundings.

### ⑧ Hide [Agility] (Equipment restriction)
The ability to hide in shadows. A success roll must be made when the thief is hiding and someone attempts to search for them or approaches. Use the same check as stealthy step.

### ⑨ Search [Intelligence] (No equipment restriction, not retryable)
The ability to search for hidden things (treasure chests with double bottoms, hidden doors in hallways, etc.). The game master determines the difficulty depending on what is hidden.

### ⑩ Climb [Agility] (Equipment restriction)
Used to climb walls and cliffs. Detailed rules are under ④ Climb in **6.6**. An owner of the thief skill (following equipment restrictions) succeeds when climbing using a rope unless they roll double ones.

### ⑪ Special Combat [Dexterity, Agility, Strength] (Equipment restriction)
Thieves can fight using their thief skill level. See **Chapter 4: Weapon Combat**. The major difference (other than equipment restrictions) is the **critical target**: a thief's attacks have a greater chance of dealing intense damage in one hit — when a thief's attack hits, the critical target is **-1** (usually 9) during the check. This modifier is always -1, regardless of skill level.

If one adventurer has acquired both the thief skill and the fighter skill, the player must choose which skill to fight with each round. You may not use different skills for attack and defense on the same round (e.g., defending with fighter, then attacking with thief). If you use weapons or armor that exceed the thief skill's restrictions, you automatically fight with the fighter skill.

### ⑫ Long Jump／High Jump [Agility] (Equipment restriction)
You can jump over obstacles using (pole) high jump or (pole) long jump. See ⑤ Long Jump／High Jump under **6.6**.

### ⑬ Follow [Agility] (Equipment restriction)
The ability to follow an opponent without them noticing. The follower uses **thief skill level + agility bonus**, the followed uses **adventurer level + intelligence bonus**. A tie is a failure. On failure, the opponent realizes they are being followed.

### ⑭ Disguise [Dexterity] (No equipment restriction)
The ability to change one's face and appearance using special makeup. It's easy to just act like a completely different person, but difficult to disguise as someone specific. Height and physique can be changed to a certain extent. If the thief is disguised as a specific person and encounters an acquaintance, a success roll determines success. The game master adds modifiers for unnaturalness (such as disguising as the opposite sex). When disguised as someone whose characteristics the thief doesn't know in detail, add a difficult modifier.

### ⑮ Treasure Appraisal [Intelligence] (No equipment restriction, not retryable)
The ability to instantly judge whether something is valuable as a treasure or not, and which one is likely to be more valuable. Also used to judge whether someone is rich based on attire, demeanor, and purse bulge. Given time, you can determine specific prices for jewelry, gold and silver, and works of art. **Magical items and books whose contents need to be understood are completely excluded.** The game master sets the target score.

### ⑯ Find／Sense Trap [Intelligence] (No equipment restriction, not retryable)
The ability to sense and find traps set indoors.

- **Find**: the thief actively identifies a location and checks if a trap is set, based on difficulty. On success, the game master accurately reveals the trap's presence and the difficulty to disarm. On failure (or if no trap is present), tell them it cannot be found. **Never say "no" even when there are no traps.**
- **Sense**: senses the presence based on past experience, without active action. Determined by a success roll just before the trap activates. Same difficulty as find, but a **-4** modifier on the player side. On success, the presence is sensed at a perilous point, and the game master must inform the player of the type of trap. Traps successfully sensed can always be found by attempting to find them.

### ⑰ Remove／Set Trap [Dexterity] (Equipment restriction, not retryable)
You can attempt to remove traps discovered through find. Removal also involves a success roll against the trap's difficulty. Unless the game master has set traps that are easy to find but difficult to remove (or vice versa), difficulties are the same. On failure, if the dice roll was **6 or more beneath the target score**, the trap activates. Traps you fail to find cannot be removed.

For **set**, you can set traps up to a difficulty equal to your thief skill level without failing (you cannot set higher-level traps). If a third party attempts to sense, find, or remove a trap set by a player, the game master attempts the success roll on their behalf.

## 6.3 Ranger Skill

Owners of the ranger skill demonstrate a variety of abilities in outdoor activities. The ranger skill is limited by equipment just like the thief skill: armor must have required strength up to half your own strength, and must be ring mail or non-metal. The ranger skill has some abilities similar to the thief skill; however, the thief skill can be used both outdoors and indoors, while the ranger skill is mostly useful only outdoors.

**Abilities:** ① Footprint Tracking, ② First Aid, ③ Camouflage, ④ Listen, ⑤ Sense Danger, ⑥ Stealthy Step, ⑦ Survivalism, ⑧ Search, ⑨ Map Creation, ⑩ Weather Prediction, ⑪ Plant and Animal Check, ⑫ Projectile Use, ⑬ Follow, ⑭ Surprise Attack, ⑮ Find／Sense Trap, ⑯ Remove／Set Trap.

### ① Footprint Tracking [Intelligence] (No equipment restriction, not retryable)
The ability to track footprints. The game master determines difficulty based on ground condition and time elapsed.

### ② First-Aid [—] (No equipment restriction)
If an adventurer's life force falls to 0 or less due to wounds, it will not recover naturally and there is a risk of death if left untreated. First-aid by a ranger restores life force to **1 point**, allowing consciousness to return. It **cannot fail**, takes about **30 minutes** of game time, and cannot be done while you can't focus (e.g., mid-combat).

### ③ Camouflage [Intelligence] (Equipment restriction)
The ability to hide behind rocks, in bushes, or use such things to hide something. Uses natural terrain; cannot be used indoors. Checks are made in the same way as the thief skill's hide.

### ④ Listen [Intelligence] (Equipment restriction)
Rangers can listen to the smallest sounds and choose sounds from a wide range. Handled the same as the thief's ability.

### ⑤ Sense Danger [Intelligence] (No equipment restriction)
A sixth sense used to sense approaching danger — e.g., seeing a pebble fall and feeling a landslide is about to occur, or feeling an arrow flying from the darkness. The target score is determined by the game master depending on the situation, but in general it should be set high.

### ⑥ Stealthy Step [Agility] (Equipment restriction)
Rangers can walk silently and unnoticed. Handled the same as the thief skill's ability.

### ⑦ Survivalism [Life Force] (No equipment restriction)
Rangers endure difficult situations — surviving with only a small amount of water, avoiding freezing in winter mountains, etc. When survival is in danger, the game master sets a target score and calls for a success roll.

### ⑧ Search [Intelligence] (No equipment restriction, not retryable)
Searching for hidden things **outdoors only** (relies on feeling out of place in nature). The game master determines difficulty.

### ⑨ Map Creation [Intelligence] (No equipment restriction, not retryable)
Rangers have a good sense of direction and can map places they've been. They can determine direction from the position of the sun or stars. A success roll determines whether the map is drawn correctly.

### ⑩ Weather Prediction [Intelligence] (No equipment restriction, not retryable)
Rangers predict weather by measuring humidity and using lore (e.g., birds eating insects flying close to the ground = no updraft = thick clouds = rain). Baseline **ranger skill level + intelligence bonus**. If successful against a set target score, the ranger predicts roughly (sunny, cloudy, rain, snow, storm, strong wind, etc.). Normally use a target score. Failure means the weather is unpredictable (and this is clear to the character too); it does not predict the opposite.

### ⑪ Plant and Animal Check [Intelligence] (No equipment restriction, not retryable)
Rangers can determine what animals and plants they see. When an animal or plant appears as a monster, make a success roll with its **rarity** as target score, similar to the sage skill's monster check. Otherwise (e.g., searching for herbs) the game master sets a target score.

### ⑫ Projectile Use [Dexterity, Strength] (No equipment restriction)
The ranger skill is treated the same as the fighter skill (attack power, bonus damage modifiers) **only when attacking with projectiles**. You can use any projectile as long as strength allows. The ranger skill is not relevant to the use of or defense from melee weapons. Adventurers attack with the ranger skill and defend with the fighter or thief skill. When using the thief skill for defense, you must be lightly armed (required strength of weapons and armor ≤ half your own strength).

### ⑬ Follow [Agility] (Equipment restriction)
Same as the thief skill's ability of the same name, but can only be used in nature — not indoors or in a city.

### ⑭ Surprise Attack [Intelligence] (No equipment restriction)
Used for surprise attacks. See **10.3: Surprise Attacks**.

### ⑮ Find／Sense Trap [Intelligence] (No equipment restriction, not retryable)
Same as the thief skill, but only for traps using outdoor nature (grass tied into a ring, strings hung from a tree, etc.). Indoor mechanical traps cannot be found／sensed with the ranger skill.

### ⑯ Remove／Set Trap [Dexterity] (Equipment restriction, not retryable)
Also follows the thief skill. Removes and creates traps using natural objects. Indoor mechanical traps cannot be removed or set.

## 6.4 Sage Skill

The sage skill represents comprehensive understanding and depth of knowledge in all academic fields. Academic fields in Sword World are undifferentiated, and owners of this skill are skilled in all disciplines. Many sorcerers have this skill. There is no requirement that you must have the sage skill and the sorcerer skill at the same time, but if you acquire both at the same time, you receive a **bonus on experience points consumed** (see **Chapter 8**).

**Abilities:** ① Monster Check, ② Languages, ③ Astrology, ④ Knowledge, ⑤ Research, ⑥ Treasure Appraisal, ⑦ Medicines.

### ① Monster Check [Intelligence] (Not retryable)
When a sage sees a monster or hears its name, this ability may reveal its true identity. Each monster has a **rarity** score. Using **sage skill level + intelligence bonus** as baseline, make a success roll with rarity as the target score; on success, you know the monster. On failure, the character has no knowledge — even if the **player** knows, treat it as if they had no prior knowledge.

### ② Languages [Intelligence]
Each time the sage skill level increases, a character can learn to **speak or read** one new language. The player chooses. **Runes cannot be chosen.** Reading and speaking are different, so becoming proficient in one language (both) needs 2 levels. You can write any language you can read and speak.

As an optional rule, you can set a target score or difficulty for text or conversations so that understanding fails on a failed success roll.

**Limitations:** Characters who cannot read **common** must first learn to read common before learning other languages. Then, a character who cannot read **low ancient** (but has learned common) must learn to read low ancient before any other language. If you can read both common and low ancient, you are free to learn subsequent languages.

For detailed rules, see **12.1: Rules Regarding Language**.

### ③ Astrology [Intelligence] (Not retryable)
From the movement of celestial bodies, you can know signs of near-future events. Signs should be given as rough information about the incident (like a newspaper headline). This ability only allows you to know about events related to the country or the world, and when no events occur, no signs appear. The game master decides what kind of sign there is; you can also set a difficulty and ask for a success roll. Solar and lunar eclipses rarely occur in Forcelia, but when they do, they are always a harbinger of something to come.

### ④ Knowledge [Intelligence] (Not retryable)
Very broad. Used for checks regarding whether you know or don't know about things in all fields, from trivia to politics to science. Two methods:

1. The game master judges whether the player knows based on sage skill level and tells them (without rarity).
2. Provide a rarity for each piece of knowledge and call for a success roll (like monster check).

Use whichever method the game master deems appropriate.

### ⑤ Research [Intelligence] (Not retryable)
When a sage fails a monster check, treasure appraisal, or knowledge check, the sage can later find out by researching literature. Research basically takes about **one week** per item (game master's discretion). During this time, you cannot do much other than research. Requires a place with a wealth of literature (best: the sorcerers' guild = academy of sages in each city). On success, the sage makes another success roll; a former failure can become a success.

### ⑥ Treasure Appraisal [Intelligence] (Not retryable)
Used to know the value and utility of treasure. The rarity becomes the target score. For standard ancient kingdom treasure (many of which are magical items), the target score is around **15**. On failure, treat it as being unable to determine the value, not as being wrong. If the true value is 100 gamels, you won't think it's 10,000 gamels just because you failed. However, if the difference from the target score is **6 or greater**, there is a possibility of such misunderstanding.

The price determined is what a connoisseur (or obsessor) would accept — the **maximum price**. If the item can be used in combat, such as weapons or armor, other adventurers may purchase it. Also, any item can be picked up by a specialty shop for **half the appraised value**. Basically, these connoisseurs and specialty shops will only exist in large towns.

### ⑦ Medicines [Intelligence] (Appraisal only: Not retryable)
Represents knowledge of poisons and medicines. Sages can appraise unknown medicines and poisons, and can mix magical potions and poisons.

- **Appraisal**: success roll with rarity as target score.
- **Creation**: game master sets target score by type. A poison able to kill in small amounts and be tasteless/odorless is around **20**. Creation requires materials and at least **one week**, regardless of result. Up to **10 doses** at most.

In Alecrast, creating and using poison is shameful and inhumane; game masters should not allow PCs to create or use poisons. See **12.2.1: Poisons**.

## 6.5 Bard Skill

Those with the bard skill are knowledgeable about ancient folklore and poetry, and make a living performing them. Bards also know information about strange lands, local legends and folklore. Each bard carries their favorite instrument and may choose any instrument, but **only one type**. Using other instruments means you cannot use abilities that require an instrument. Famous instruments include the lyre and the lute.

**Abilities:** ① Sing, ② Play Instrument, ③ Languages (Spoken), ④ Spellsongs, ⑤ Folklore Knowledge.

### ① Sing [Mental Power]
The art of singing. Most songs are melodies attached to legends and folklore — heroic tales and love poems.

### ② Play Instrument [Dexterity]
The art of playing a musical instrument. The higher the bard's level, the more skillful.

Bards can earn **bard skill level × bard skill level × 2D silver coins** by singing and playing for about an hour in a suitable location (such as a tavern).

### ③ Languages (Spoken) [Intelligence]
Almost the same as the sage's ability, but only **speaking**, not reading. Bards can converse in languages other than their native tongue, but cannot read or write. Unlike the sage skill, there is **no restriction** on the languages that can be learned — but you cannot learn runes.

For detailed rules, see **12.1: Rules Regarding Language**.

### ④ Spellsongs [Mental Power]
A **spellsong** is a magical song from the ancient kingdom period that, when played with a melody on an instrument or by singing a poem, produces an effect similar to a spell. Musical instruments are always required. Being able to sing is also required, with one exception: if you choose a **flute** as your instrument. A bard can learn one spellsong per skill level. See **5.3: Spellsongs**.

### ⑤ Folklore Knowledge [Intelligence] (Not retryable)
A bard is familiar with myths and folklore passed down in various regions, as well as ancient poetry. Some legends may serve as clues — a saga of battle between a monster and a hero may contain information about the monster's weakness; legends of the ancient kingdom may lead adventurers to ruins. If the game master wants to provide information as ancient poems or folklore, decide on a **rarity** as the target score for a success roll.

## 6.6 Common Adventurer Abilities

Adventurers naturally acquire various abilities due to their adventurous lifestyle. These **common adventurer abilities** use **adventurer level + related ability bonus** as the baseline score.

**Abilities:** ① Riding, ② Swim, ③ Ship Steering, ④ Climb, ⑤ Long Jump／High Jump.

### ① Riding [Agility]
If just riding normally, no roll needed. When jumping over obstacles, engaging in combat, or during similarly intense movements (every round during combat), use **adventurer level + agility bonus** vs target score **7**. If you try to control your horse without using the reins, suffer **-4**. On failure, you fall off — treated as falling from **3 meters** (see ④ Climb for damage). Though a horse isn't 3 m tall, this accounts for speed and impact. Those with the thief skill can reduce damage using tumble; an average thief should avoid any damage.

These rules apply to **trained horses** (riding horses, war horses). For a **wild horse**, you must first succeed on a success roll to mount: target score **9**, but wild horses have no reins (**-4 penalty**) — effectively **13**. To control a wild horse in combat, target score **11** (effectively **15** with no reins).

### ② Swim [Agility]
Basically all adventurers can swim. You can swim across any pond about **25 meters** in size with no roll. For an island many kilometers away, or swimming across rapids, a success roll is needed against a target set by the game master — baseline **adventurer level + agility bonus**. Failure means drowning.

You can only wear **cloth or at most soft leather** to swim. Any other armor and you sink immediately.

**Removing armor underwater:** succeed on a success roll using **adventurer level + dexterity bonus**. Target scores: **13** for ring mail, hard leather, and mithril chain mail; **15** for chain mail; **19** for lamellar, splint, and plate armor. On success you can take it off and rise. This attempt takes **one minute**, regardless of success. An adventurer can only hold breath underwater for a number of rounds equal to their life force (10 s each), so this roll can be made at most **life force ÷ 6** times (your life force bonus). If you can't take the armor off in time, you drown.

Heavy weapons and luggage also impede swimming. Recklessly swimming with a sword, spear, or ax: **adventurer level + agility bonus** vs target score **13** (adjust for weight, distance, or rapids). On failure you sink; unlike armor, weapons are easily discarded.

When a drowning person loses consciousness, life force immediately falls to 0 — a **death check** is required. If alive, they can breathe again if rescued and treated within **30 minutes**. After 30 minutes without rescue, they automatically die.

### ③ Ship Steering [Dexterity]
Adventurers can handle small boats using oars or poles. **Sailing ships require the sailor skill**, and adventurer level cannot substitute. For calm water traveling straight, no roll needed. For avoiding collision or surviving rapids: **adventurer level + dexterity bonus** vs difficulty set by the GM. On failure, the boat capsizes.

### ④ Climb [Agility]
Climbing is both a common ability and a thief ability (the latter allows more advanced challenges).

- **Common adventurer climb**: always requires assistance such as rope. Without such a thing, it's not possible to climb using adventurer level as baseline.
- **Thief skill climb**: does not require rope. With a rope, you almost certainly climb — only **double ones** fail.

See **Table 6-1**. When a thief climbs without a rope, or a non-thief climbs with a rope, set target score using **Table 6-2** and make a success roll. Baseline is **thief skill level + agility bonus** or **adventurer level + agility bonus**. When a thief climbs up or down a wall or cliff without rope, conditions marked ***** are not possible.

**Table 6-1: Climb by Skill**

| | With Rope | Without Rope |

If he is a thief, he can discard the heavy armor and use the thief skill — only double ones fail.

**Example 1:** Climbing a **22 m** cliff: 3 checks. First two succeed, third fails. Rolls 2D = 5 → 5-2 = 3 m added to 20 m = 23 m. Cliff is climbed.

**Example 2:** If the 2D for the final-stretch distance rolled double ones or 3, the adventurer would've fallen from 20 or 21 m.

**Falling Damage.** If an adventurer falls:

- **Base damage = fall height × 3** to life force.
- **Reduction**: make a **defense roll** using armor's defense power as the key number, add adventurer level. On **double ones**, all reduction (including adventurer level) is lost.

> **Falling damage = fall height × 3 − (defense roll result + adventurer level)**

Base damage × 3 assumes normal ground. For soft grass or water surface, reduce the factor (× 2 or × 1). For spikes, etc., increase the factor.

Owners of the **thief skill** greatly reduce damage with tumble: subtract **thief skill level + agility bonus (in meters)** from the fall height. If 0 or less, the thief lands cleanly on their feet.

**Example:** Thief skill 3, dexterity bonus +2 → fall from 5 m or less takes no damage. Above 6 m, base damage = **(fall height − 5) × 3**.

# Chapter 7: General Skills

*(Source txt lines 7865–8067)*

Even non-player characters with adventurer level 0 have some skills. For example, a farmer might have the **farmer skill**, a barman might have the **bartender skill**, and so on. Being proficient in these skills is of no use when fighting monsters or searching ruins. Skills not directly related to adventure are called **general skills**.

## 7.1 Notes on General Skills

### The Difference Between General Skills and Adventurer Skills

The hunter skill is very similar to the ranger skill — their abilities overlap quite a bit, and a good ranger can also be a good hunter. The reason the two must be separated is that the **hunter skill is a general skill**, while the **ranger skill is an adventurer skill**.

General skills are the skills necessary for daily life. Adventurer skills are the necessary skills to live as an adventurer. The biggest difference is whether you can become a **hero**. In Sword World RPG, adventurer level increases as an adventurer's skill level increases — adventurer level can even be called **hero level**.

A good ranger has the potential to be called a hero; a hunter, no matter how good, has a hard time being recognized as one. Being a hunter is a respectable profession and people with higher general skill levels deserve due respect. They just aren't called heroes. That said, for exceptional cases (like William Tell the hunter shooting the apple), the GM may grant adventurer skills to masters of general skills.

### How to Create General Skills

There are as many general skills as there are occupations and hobbies. The book only introduces the necessary ones; the GM may codify others as needed:

1. **Decide on the purpose** of the skill (e.g., we need a skill for fishermen).
2. **Decide on a name** for the skill (e.g., **fisher skill**).
3. **Consider restrictions** for using the skill (e.g., fishermen probably don't need any).
4. **Think about the abilities** required (e.g., a skilled fisherman must identify fish — *fish check*; be good at swimming — *swim*; be able to operate a boat — *small ship steering*).

## 7.2 Merchant Skill

Related to trade. Merchants are a class with a lot of freedom in Sword World, often traveling — including **peddlers** who are always on the move. Merchants are usually issued a **merchant's pass** by the king, granting the right to open a shop in public. Adventurers with this skill can sell items obtained from adventures in shops or on the street. Without the certificate you cannot conduct transactions publicly (but you may freely buy, sell, or transfer personal items).

Merchants tend to specialize (arts, fresh produce, etc.) and are no different from ordinary people outside their specialty. However, the basics of trade do transfer.

**Abilities:** ① Value Check, ② Negotiation.

### ① Value Check [Intelligence] (Not retryable)
Almost the same as the thief and sage skills' treasure appraisal, but only for items the merchant deals with. An arts merchant tells you the value of art; a weapons merchant tells you the value of weapons/armor. Result is a specific price in silver coins.

### ② Negotiation [Mental Power] (Not retryable)
In Alecrast, merchants generally purchase items at **half the retail price** — a 100-gamel product is bought from a craftsman for 50. Using this ability, the merchant can take stock at a lower price or sell at a higher price.

To use it, you must know the correct price of the item. Each side makes a success roll using **merchant level + mental power bonus** as baseline. This common merchant ability applies even to products outside your specialty. The side with the higher final score gets terms more favorable to them. Either side can refuse, but that is poor business ethics.

The price changes by about **3% per 1-point difference** in final score. Round up fractions.

**Example:** Ducard Ⅱ tries to sell an ancient kingdom vase (price 100 gamels) to an antique dealer. Ducard has no merchant skill → baseline 0. Antique dealer: merchant level 5, mental power +2. Ducard rolls 2D=8 → final 8. Dealer rolls 2D=9 → final 9+5+2=16. Difference 8 → 24% off the normal purchase price of 50 gamels = **38 gamels**.

## 7.3 Craftsman Skill

In Sword World, all production is done by hand — quality depends on the craftsman. Products are picked up by merchants and sold in shops. Higher-skill craftsmen's items are more expensive. Craftsmen must belong to a **guild**; it is illegal for anyone outside a guild to make products handled by that guild.

**Abilities:** ① Craft, ② Value Check.

### ① Craft [Dexterity]
Creating items. Given appropriate time (varies by price/quality), a craftsman creates a product that complies with their level. Depending on the item you may need tools and equipment. Merchants pick up the product at **half the price list**. Higher-level craftsmen make higher-quality goods; the GM decides bonuses for expensive high-quality items.

**Success rolls** are not required for normal-quality items. Weapon and armor craftsmen need a success roll to create **high-quality weapons and armor**: target score = **15 + the decrease in required strength**. However, you **cannot** create weapons/armor with required strength of **6 or lower**.

### ② Value Check [Intelligence] (Not retryable)
Craftsmen know their own products' quality and can determine the value of items made by others. Treated the same as the merchant's value check.

## 7.4 Healer Skill

Since magic exists, medicine has not advanced much. With few rune masters around, **healers** usually heal injuries and illnesses. Healers know basic medicine and herbs.

**Abilities:** ① First Aid, ② Treatment, ③ Herbs.

### ① First-Aid [—]
Same as the ranger skill's ability of the same name.

### ② Treatment [Intelligence]
Treats illnesses and injuries and aids natural recovery. Normally, natural damage recovery is 1 point per day. With a healer nursing, use the **Rating Table** with **healer skill level** as key number — the patient recovers life force equal to the result plus the healer's **intelligence bonus**. On **double ones**, no recovery at all. Takes at least **one hour**. Illnesses: see **12.2.2**.

### ③ Herbs [Intelligence] (Not retryable)
Discerning herbs and minerals effective for treatment. Make a success roll with **rarity** as the target score. Also used to make medicine by mixing herbs. See **12.2.3: Herbs**.

## 7.5 Hunter Skill

Needed to hunt. People called hunters own this skill, and owners are called hunters. Even those hunting as a hobby/side job have this skill. Subject to the same **equipment restrictions** as the ranger skill. Overlapping abilities follow the ranger skill.

**Abilities:** ① Footprint Tracking, ② Find／Sense Trap, ③ Remove／Set Trap, ④ Stealthy Step, ⑤ Camouflage, ⑥ Listen, ⑦ Plant and Animal Check, ⑧ Projectile Use, ⑨ Pre-processing, ⑩ Value Check.

Abilities ① through ⑧ are exactly the same as the ranger's.

### ⑨ Pre-processing [Dexterity]
Pre-processing prey into sellable product — separating antlers from deer carcasses, stripping and tanning hides, taking apart and smoking meat, etc. On failure, product value drops to half normal. The GM sets a target score per attempt. Cutting off horns may not be hard, but skinning small animals and skillfully smoking meat can be quite difficult.

### ⑩ Value Check [Intelligence] (Not retryable)
Unlike rangers, hunters turn prey into gold. Determines the value of prey and pre-processed items against a GM-set difficulty. The GM should not set a very high difficulty unless the animal is extremely rare.

## 7.6 Sailor Skill

Needed to operate a ship. Even those sailing as a hobby/side job have this skill. Some abilities require **very light equipment** — only cloth or soft leather armor.

**Abilities:** ① Large Ship Steering, ② Seamanship, ③ Small Ship Steering, ④ Swim, ⑤ Weather Prediction, ⑥ Climb, ⑦ Ropework.

### ① Large Ship Steering [Intelligence]
**Large ships** (fishing, trading, battleships) cannot be operated by one person — at least **half** the crew must have the sailor skill. For delicate maneuvers (surviving a storm, avoiding a reef, ramming), the helmsman succeeds on a roll with **sailor skill level + intelligence bonus** baseline. The captain can give instructions using their own sailor skill level + intelligence bonus. Failure won't sink a large ship immediately but will likely cause damage.

### ② Seamanship [Intelligence]
Knowing direction by the stars, identifying dangerous reef areas, etc. Use **sailor skill level + intelligence bonus** baseline vs GM-set difficulty.

### ③ Small Ship Steering [Dexterity]
Handling small boats moved by poles or oars. Calm straight water: no roll. Avoiding collision or surviving rapids: **sailor skill level + dexterity bonus** vs GM-set difficulty. On failure, capsize.

### ④ Swim [Agility] (Equipment restriction)
Follows the **Swim** common adventurer ability, but replace adventurer level with **sailor skill level**.

### ⑤ Weather Prediction [Intelligence] (Not retryable)
Sailors predict changes in weather at sea. See the ranger ability of the same name.

### ⑥ Climb [Agility] (Equipment restriction)
Ascending masts or nets. Handled the same as the thief skill's ability.

### ⑦ Ropework [Dexterity]
Tying ropes so they don't unravel, or untying quickly. A sailor unties a knot by succeeding on **sailor skill level + dexterity bonus** vs GM-set difficulty (considering tightness and time). A rope a sailor tied to stay fast has **difficulty equal to the sailor's skill level** for anyone trying to untie it.

# Chapter 8: Character Growth

*(Source txt lines 8068–8227)*

As you adventure and accumulate experience, your character advances and becomes stronger. This chapter explains the rules for character growth.

## 8.1 Experience Points

**Experience points** indicate how much experience the character has gained from adventures. Character growth is done using these experience points.

## 8.2 How to Use Experience Points: Increasing Skill Levels

At the end of an adventure, characters gain experience points. By spending them, a character increases their skill level — you pay experience points to buy skill levels. A character can advance in any number of skills and by any number of levels at once. However, a character's skill level can only reach **10th level**.

The EXP required is shown in **Table 8-1**. Ignore the rightmost column (sorcerer & sage reduction) for now.

**Table 8-1: Experience Points by Skill**

| Attained Level | Sorcerer | Shaman / Dragon Priest | Fighter / Thief / Priest / Dark Priest | Ranger / Sage / Bard | Reduction for Acquiring Sorcerer & Sage at the Same Time |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 2,000 | 1,500 | 1,000 | 500 | -500 |
| 2 | 3,000 | 2,000 | 1,500 | 1,000 | -500 |
| 3 | 4,000 | 3,000 | 2,000 | 1,500 | -1,000 |
| 4 | 5,000 | 4,000 | 3,000 | 2,000 | -1,000 |
| 5 | 7,000 | 5,000 | 4,000 | 3,000 | -1,500 |
| 6 | 9,000 | 7,000 | 5,000 | 4,000 | -2,000 |
| 7 | 12,000 | 9,000 | 7,000 | 5,000 | -3,000 |
| 8 | 15,000 | 12,000 | 9,000 | 7,000 | -4,000 |
| 9 | 20,000 | 15,000 | 12,000 | 9,000 | -5,000 |
| 10 | 25,000 | 20,000 | 15,000 | 12,000 | -6,000 |

**Example:** Ducard Ⅱ gains 5,000 EXP. He raises fighter 2 → 3 (2,000 pts), leaving 3,000. He raises ranger 1 → 3 in one go (1,000 + 1,500 = 2,500 pts), leaving **500** EXP.

Remaining EXP can be carried over and added to the next adventure's EXP. This may seem strange; think of it as cutting off growth by the amount carried.

**Example:** Ducard Ⅱ keeps 500 EXP. If he gains 5,000 again next adventure, he has **5,500** to use.

EXP cannot be negative — you can't grow from experiences you haven't had. You also cannot sell your skill levels for EXP.

### 8.2.1 Sorcerer Skill and Sage Skill

The sorcerer skill is mastery of ancient through study and magical ability; the sage skill is knowledge gained primarily through ancient books. Both involve the study of ancient and ancient books — many sorcerers are excellent sages, and the sorcerers' guild is also called the **academy of sages**.

When a character already has sorcerer and acquires sage at the same level or lower, or vice versa, **EXP spent is reduced** per the rightmost column of Table 8-1.

**Example 1:** Sorcerer 3 / Sage 1 → raising sage to 2. Sage 2 is lower than sorcerer 3, so reduction applies. Reduction for level 2 is 500 → EXP = 1,000 − 500 = **500**. Raising sage to 3 likewise: 1,500 − 1,000 = **500**. No reduction at sage 4, because sorcerer is only 3.

**Example 2:** Sorcerer 1 / Sage 3 → raising sorcerer. Sorcerer 2: 3,000 − 500 = **2,500**. Sorcerer 3: 4,000 − 1,000 = **3,000**. No reduction at sorcerer 4. A character with sorcerer 3 or sage 3 gets no reduction at level 4.

**Example 3:** A character with neither skill acquires sorcerer 1 → can also acquire sage 1 at the same time **without spending EXP**. Acquiring sorcerer 1 first gives sage 1 at 500 − 500 = **0**.

## 8.3 How to Use Experience Points: Increasing Ability Scores

EXP can also raise ability scores. **+1 to dexterity, agility, intelligence, or strength** costs **3,000 EXP for humans**, **5,000 EXP for non-human adventurers**. **Life force and mental power** are **half** that (1,500 / 2,500). No maximum.

## 8.4 Increasing General Skills

General skills are not necessary for adventurers, but may lead to individualization and expand role-playing.

General skills **cannot** be purchased with EXP like adventurer skills. It's only acceptable to increase a general skill if you've used that specific skill for a long period. All decisions are made by the GM.

For example: a character forced to work in a bar for two months as punishment for eating and drinking without paying learns how to be liked at a bar and make simple dishes that pair with drinks. The GM might grant **2 levels** in bartender. Suitable for the job? **3 levels**. Unsuitable? **1 level**.

**5th level** signifies being sufficient at a general skill; reaching beyond takes a large amount of effort. A casual free-time hobby probably caps around 3rd level. Few humans reach 10th level in general skills.
